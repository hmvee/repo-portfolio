## About the piece

"The Unmapped Hour" started as a note I wrote during a long video call when I
realized I had no idea what time it was — not just the hour, but whether it was
morning or afternoon. The experience lasted only a second, but it stuck with me.

The essay uses that moment as a lens for examining how much of our sense of
"being productive" was actually borrowed from the environment: the commute that
forced a transition, the lunch queue that imposed a break, the colleague who left
at six and made it socially acceptable to do the same.

## Structure

The piece is organized in four movements:

**I. The calibrated life** — An account of how pre-distributed-work environments
structured time without us needing to structure it ourselves. The office as a
clock you could ignore.

**II. The collapse of transitions** — What happens to cognition and emotional
regulation when the environmental cues that mark the passage of time disappear.
Drawing on work by cognitive scientists studying attention and context switching.

**III. The invention problem** — Why "just create your own structure" is harder
than it sounds, and what distinguishes people who manage it from those who don't.
(Spoiler: it has less to do with discipline than with having reasons to mark time
that feel real.)

**IV. The unmapped hour** — A more speculative close: what a genuinely good
relationship with unstructured time might look like, and why I think we've
been measuring the wrong thing when we evaluate remote work's impact on
"productivity."

## Reception

The piece was read by about 4,000 people in its first week, mostly from a link
shared in a workplace futures newsletter. The responses that meant the most to
me were from people who said it named something they'd been feeling but hadn't
been able to articulate.

I'm not a professional writer — I design things for a living. But writing this
piece changed how I think about the products I build, particularly the ones
that operate at the intersection of work and attention.
